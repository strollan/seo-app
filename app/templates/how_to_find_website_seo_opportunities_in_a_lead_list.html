Export a list of local businesses and you hit the next problem fast: not every name on it is worth calling. The list might come from a scraper, a data provider, a Chamber of Commerce directory, or an old CRM. However it was built, some of those businesses have solid websites and show up in search. Others have broken pages, thin service information, or a site you can't find even when you type in the exact business name. Working out which entries deserve a closer look, before you spend time on outreach, is research work more than sales work.

Why This Matters More Than List Size

You already have the names. What you don't have yet is a reason to call any particular one. That's the gap this process closes. It separates businesses with a website or search problem worth checking from the ones that give you nothing specific to say.

A list of 500 businesses sounds like 500 chances. It isn't. Call them in the order they exported and you spend the same effort on a company with a clean site and a full Google Business Profile as on one with real problems. A shorter list, where every row has an observation attached to it, lets each call start with the business in front of you instead of the same generic opening.

The question to keep asking isn't "does this business need marketing help?" That question is too broad to help you prioritize. The useful version is narrower: is there a website or search issue I can actually point to, and does it relate to work I offer?

Step 1: Confirm the Website Actually Exists and Loads

Before anything resembling SEO analysis, check that there's a working site attached to a real business:

  • The domain resolves. It isn't parked, expired, or pointed somewhere unrelated.
  • The site loads on desktop and on a phone, with no errors.
  • It has an SSL certificate, and the browser isn't flagging it as not secure.
  • The business still operates under this name and hasn't closed, rebranded, or been acquired.

Two different questions are hiding in that list, and it matters which one you're answering. Whether the business is still real, and whether the website is any good. Only the first one takes a row off the list.

Filter out an entry only when it's genuinely out of scope: the business has closed, the listing points to the wrong business entirely, or the name and contact details don't match a real, currently operating entity. A list pulled from a scrape or an older directory will always carry a few of these.

Don't filter out an active business just because its website is missing, broken, parked, or hard to find. That isn't a disqualification. It's a possible opportunity that still needs verifying. Confirm the business is genuinely open through another channel, such as a phone call, a current listing, or recent review activity, then look at whether the work you offer fits what the business needs. Either way, that row carries forward into the later steps.

Step 2: Assess Baseline Website Quality

With the site confirmed live, look for structural and usability problems that are visible without any specialized tooling.

  • Mobile layout. Does it break, overlap, or force pinch-zooming on a phone?
  • Page speed. Does it feel sluggish on a standard connection? A manual check just flags pages worth measuring later in a tool like PageSpeed Insights.
  • Content freshness. Old copyright years, dated references, service pages describing offerings the business may no longer provide.
  • Contact and conversion paths. A phone number, address, and contact form that are easy to find, or essential details buried.
  • Update consistency. Do service details, hours, contact information, and dated notices agree across pages, or has an update reached only part of the site?

None of this proves a business is losing revenue. Plenty of companies with unremarkable websites do fine through referrals, foot traffic, and reputation. What you get is a documented starting point you can explain to a non-technical owner without promising an outcome.

Step 3: Check Search Visibility for Their Own Business Name

Run a branded search: the business name plus its city or service area. Write down what comes back.

  • Does their website appear in the top few results, or is it buried below directory listings, review sites, and competitors?
  • Does their Google Business Profile show up with accurate hours, categories, and photos, or is it thin, unclaimed, or out of step with the website?
  • Are duplicate or outdated listings competing with the real one?

If the website is hard to find in one branded search, record exactly what appeared and repeat the check before you use it to set priority. A weak result doesn't always mean the website is the problem. A new domain, a recent rebrand, a Google Business Profile issue, or ordinary variation in search results can each produce it. Confirm the cause before you assume one.

Step 4: Look for Service and Location-Specific Gaps

Here's a pattern worth watching for. A business does strong work across several services or towns but never built pages targeting those specific terms, so the website doesn't clearly represent everything the business actually offers.

  • Does the site have a dedicated page for each core service, or does everything funnel into one generic "services" page with a paragraph per offering?
  • If the business serves multiple towns or neighborhoods, are there location-specific pages, or does the site only reference one central address?
  • Do service and location terms appear in page titles, headings, and body copy, or only in a footer service-area list?

Find that pattern and you have a specific question to investigate rather than a vague "you need more SEO" pitch.

Step 5: Spot-Check Technical SEO Fundamentals

You don't need a full technical audit at the lead-qualification stage. A few quick checks separate genuine issues from cosmetic ones.

  • Title tags and meta descriptions. Present, unique per page, and reasonably descriptive, or missing and duplicated across the site? Page source or a free browser extension will show you.
  • Indexing status. A site:domain.com search in Google is a rough, informal check rather than a reliable page count. If it returns close to nothing, dig into that further, with Search Console if you have access, rather than treating it as confirmed proof the site isn't indexed.
  • Broken links or 404s. Click a few obvious internal links. Do they work?
  • Structured data. Any schema markup for the business, or none at all?

Each of these is a factual, checkable item. None of it, on its own, is a diagnosis of why traffic or leads might be low.

Step 6: Compare Against Local Competitors, Not Abstract Benchmarks

A generic SEO score means little to a local business owner. Pull up two or three other businesses in the same city offering the same service, and compare this one against those.

  • Do competitors have more reviews, more service pages, or higher-ranking content for the same core keywords?
  • Is a competitor's Google Business Profile clearly more complete?
  • Are competitors running visible content, such as blog posts, project galleries, or FAQs, that this business isn't?

Set an isolated observation next to a few direct local competitors and it gains context. Record the difference you found. Don't claim it explains their search performance or guarantees a fix.

Step 7: Prioritize the List Based on What You Found

Once you've worked through a batch, sort the entries into rough tiers rather than trying to rank every business precisely.

  1. High-priority observations. Dead links, missing service pages, or weak branded-search visibility. A possible indexing issue that still needs verifying. Or an active business with no usable website at all. That last case is the strongest signal here. It only counts once you've confirmed through another channel that the business is genuinely open and operating. Recheck the whole tier before using any of it in outreach.
  2. Moderate usability or content gaps. Thin service information, hard-to-find contact paths, inconsistent updates, or an incomplete Google Business Profile. Worth further research, with less urgent framing.
  3. Few actionable findings. Functioning site, useful service information, no meaningful search issue in your checks. Deprioritize unless you have a genuinely different angle to offer.
  4. Disqualified. The business itself is closed, the listing points to the wrong business entirely, or the contact information doesn't match a real, currently operating entity. A confirmed-active business without a usable website belongs in Tier 1, not here.

That turns a flat list into a workable research order, with a recorded observation ready for each conversation instead of a generic pitch.

A Note on What This Process Doesn't Tell You

Everything above relies on public observations. It doesn't measure actual lead volume, conversion rate, or revenue impact for a business you haven't worked with yet.

So don't translate "this site has technical issues" into "this business is losing X leads per month" without evidence. That's a claim you can't support from outside research, and business owners who've heard inflated pitches before will notice the difference between an observation and a guess dressed up as a statistic.
